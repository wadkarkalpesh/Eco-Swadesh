Industry Overview 4.1 Market size and growth [Verified] The global organic farming market was valued at roughly $231.89 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow to $257.85 billion in 2026, a compound annual growth rate near 11.2%, with growth expected to continue toward $404.4 billion by 2030. A separate but consistent estimate from the same research house’s organic food market coverage puts the organic food market at about $255 billion in 2025, rising to roughly $286 billion in 2026 and projected to reach $719.5 billion by 2034 at a 12.2% CAGR . A third independent estimate is directionally similar, projecting organic foods reaching roughly $253.6 billion in 2026 and $673.3 billion by 2034 . Regional distribution matters for Eco Swadesh’s global-by-design mandate: Europe was the largest regional market in 2025 at roughly $99.9 billion (about 13% global share), followed by North America at roughly $89.8 billion (about 12.75% share), with Asia-Pacific at about $26.1 billion and growing . Within Asia, India’s organic market alone is projected at roughly $1.7 billion in 2026 — small in absolute dollar terms but structurally important because of farmer population size. For context on the underlying farmland base, FAO data cited in industry reporting indicates more than 75 million hectares worldwide are now under organic agricultural management , and earlier FAO-linked figures described organic agriculture as practiced in 187 countries with roughly 72.3 million hectares managed organically by at least 3.1 million farmers as of the early 2020s — meaning the certified organic farmer population, while large in absolute terms, is a small fraction of the world’s ~500+ million farms, most of which are smallholders who farm sustainably in practice but are neither certified nor digitally reachable. The primary authoritative longitudinal source for this data is FiBL/IFOAM’s annual publication: “ The World of Organic Agriculture, ” now in its 27th edition, published by the Research Institute of Organic Agriculture (FiBL) and IFOAM – Organics International, which compiles area under organic management, farm counts, retail sales, and trade data across regions . This should be the team’s standing reference for all market-sizing decisions going forward, refreshed annually. 4.2 Structural characteristics of the industry Characteristic Implication for a platform like Eco Swadesh Highly fragmented supply side (millions of smallholders) No single supplier relationship scales; onboarding and trust-building must be self-serve and low-friction Certification is regional and non-interoperable (USDA NOP, EU Organic Regulation, India NPOP/PGS-India, JAS, etc.) A global platform needs a certification abstraction layer, not a single hard-coded “ organic ” checkbox High information asymmetry between producer and buyer Trust and verification become the core product, not a feature Thin retail margins for smallholders, high consumer price premiums Value is being captured by intermediaries — a disintermediation opportunity exists but must be handled carefully (see Porter’s analysis, §8) Growth outpacing overall agriculture (~11–12% CAGR organic vs. [Verified] 6.6–6.7% CAGR for agriculture overall ) Organic/sustainable is not a niche — it is gaining share of the total agriculture market 5. Current Market Problems 5.1 Problem clusters Trust and verification gap. Buyers cannot easily verify that a product marketed as “ organic ” or “ sustainable ” actually is, especially across borders where certification schemes differ. Fragmented, single-purpose tooling. Farmers today juggle a marketplace app for inputs, a separate app for crop diagnosis, a separate community/Instagram-style channel for peer learning, and offline or phone-based channels for selling produce. No single trusted identity follows the farmer across all of these. Producer disintermediation is incomplete. Even “ direct-to-farmer ” models remain input-side (selling seeds/fertilizer to farmers) rather than output-side (helping farmers sell verified produce to the world), leaving the sales-side problem largely unsolved. Geographic and language exclusion. Most strong platforms are single-country or single-language optimized (e.g., Hindi/regional-language India apps), which blocks a genuinely global producer or buyer base. Knowledge is commoditized advertising, not independent expertise. Advisory content in many agri-apps is tied to input sales (recommend the product the platform sells), rather than neutral agronomy guidance. No unified reputation/trust layer for individuals. A “ verified organic gardener ” or “ trusted small-batch producer ” has no portable, cross-platform reputation, unlike, say, a seller rating on Etsy or Amazon. 5.2 Pain points by stakeholder Stakeholder Pain point Smallholder / organic producer Cannot reach buyers beyond local mandi/market; certification costs and paperwork are prohibitive; no digital storefront; advisory is sales-biased Home/urban gardener No trusted source to buy verified organic inputs or sell surplus produce; fragmented, ad-driven content on plant care Diaspora / ethical consumer Wants to buy verified sustainable/organic goods from home-country producers but faces logistics, trust, and payment barriers Small sustainable brand/business Needs a channel to reach global customers without building e-commerce infrastructure or fighting Amazon/Alibaba-scale competition for visibility Agronomist / expert No monetizable, reputation-building channel to advise farmers directly; expertise is diluted across social media Certification body / regulator No easy way to make verified certification status machine-readable and checkable by consumers at point of purchase 5.3 Root causes Certification systems were designed for regulatory compliance and export documentation, not consumer-facing digital trust (e.g., a QR-code-verifiable, real-time certification status). Agri-tech investment to date has concentrated on input supply chain efficiency (BigHaat, AgroStar) or commodity/carbon markets (Indigo Ag) because those have clearer near-term unit economics, leaving the consumer-facing trust marketplace layer underbuilt. Global marketplaces (Amazon, Alibaba, Etsy) are optimized for volume and breadth, not for domain-specific verification of organic/sustainability claims — trust signals are generic star ratings, not certification-grade data. Government and NGO organic-promotion programs are typically national in scope (USDA NOP, EU Organic Regulation, India’s NPOP), which structurally prevents any single one from becoming a global consumer brand. 5.4 Problem tree [Diagram — Mermaid source shown below; render with any Mermaid-compatible viewer for the visual] flowchart TB ROOT[&quot;Core problem: Trusted global market access for&lt;br/&gt;sustainable &amp; organic agriculture does not exist&quot;] C1[&quot;Effect: Producers stay local /&lt;br/&gt;underpaid / invisible to global buyers&quot;] C2[&quot;Effect: Consumers cannot verify&lt;br/&gt;authenticity of &#39;organic&#39; or &#39;sustainable&#39; claims&quot;] C3[&quot;Effect: Expertise stays informal,&lt;br/&gt;unmonetized, and sales-biased&quot;] C4[&quot;Effect: Sustainable agri-businesses&lt;br/&gt;can&#39;t scale beyond local/offline reach&quot;] ROOT --&gt; C1 ROOT --&gt; C2 ROOT --&gt; C3 ROOT --&gt; C4 R1[&quot;Root cause: No unified,&lt;br/&gt;cross-border digital trust layer&quot;] R2[&quot;Root cause: Certification systems&lt;br/&gt;are national, paper-based, non-interoperable&quot;] R3[&quot;Root cause: Existing platforms are&lt;br/&gt;single-purpose (buy inputs OR diagnose OR chat)&quot;] R4[&quot;Root cause: Agri-tech investment favors&lt;br/&gt;input supply chain &amp; carbon markets over&lt;br/&gt;consumer trust marketplaces&quot;] C1 --&gt; R1 C2 --&gt; R2 C3 --&gt; R3 C4 --&gt; R4 5.5 Who suffers (impact ranking, most to least severe) Smallholder organic/sustainable producers — bear the highest cost of certification and the least market power. Vision Connect every organic producer, supplier, expert, gardener, business, and customer through one trusted digital ecosystem. This is deliberately a connection vision, not a transaction vision. Mission Empower sustainable agriculture through technology by making trusted products, verified knowledge, and global communities accessible. Three verbs matter here and should be treated as product pillars, not marketing copy: - Trusted products → the marketplace and certification/verification layer. - Verified knowledge → the AI assistant and expert community, explicitly designed to resist the sales-biased advisory pattern identified in Document 1, §5.1.4. - Global communities → the social/community layer, designed region-agnostic from day one. 6. Core Values Value What it means in product decisions Trust over scale We will grow slower than a generic marketplace if that’s what verification integrity requires. A false “ verified organic ” badge is a worse outcome than a missing badge. Farmer/producer dignity Producers are represented as professionals and experts, not as commodity SKUs. Reputation is portable and earned. Global by default, local by design Every feature must work in at least two currencies, two certification regimes, and two languages before it ships to the first market, even if only one is live at launch. Neutral expertise Advisory content and AI assistant answers must be separable from sponsored/paid input listings, always disclosed when not. Radical transparency in claims Sustainability and organic claims are never platform-asserted without a verifiable source; unverified claims are labeled as such, not hidden. 7. Product Principles One identity, many roles. A single Eco Swadesh account can be a buyer, seller, and community member simultaneously — mirroring how real smallholders and gardeners actually operate (grow some, buy some, sell some, ask questions about all of it). Certification is a first-class data object , not a text field — it has an issuing authority, a validity window, a scope, and a verification method, so the platform can support USDA NOP, EU Organic Regulation, India NPOP/PGS-India, JAS, and future schemes without a data-model rewrite. AI assists, humans verify. AI-driven crop diagnosis and recommendations (table stakes per Document 1 — Plantix, BigHaat already ship this) are treated as a starting hypothesis for the user, with a clear path to a human expert, not a replacement for one. Design for the next country before you need it. Currency, units (metric/imperial), date formats, address formats, and language are abstracted from day one, per the founding brief’s explicit “ no redesign for expansion ” mandate. Community before critical mass. Early community features (expert Q&amp;A, grower stories) are seeded intentionally in the launch market rather than left empty, avoiding the “ empty room ” failure mode of new marketplaces. 8. Business Goals Horizon Goal Year 1 Prove the trust/verification model and producer-side supply liquidity in the India launch market, anchored by a diaspora/ethical-consumer demand wedge (per Document 1, §7) Year 2–3 Expand to a second and third market (candidates: USA/UK diaspora corridor, Middle East, South East Asia) without structural rework Year 3–5 Establish Eco Swadesh’s trust score as a recognized cross-border signal, potentially licensed to third parties (certification-as-a-service) Year 5–10 Become a default reference point for “ is this actually organic/sustainable ” the way a credit score is a default reference point for creditworthiness 9. Why this metric and not GMV: GMV rewards growth even if verification is skipped or gamed — the exact failure mode of existing marketplaces identified in Document 1, §6. VTT forces trust and commerce to grow together, which is the actual differentiation thesis. 9.2 Supporting metrics Metric What it tracks % of active listings with a machine-verifiable certification or platform-issued trust score Health of the trust layer Producer repeat-listing rate (30/90 day) Whether producers find enough value to keep participating Cross-border transaction share Whether the “ global by design ” thesis is materializing, not just aspirational Expert-answered questions per active community member Health of the neutral-expertise pillar vs. sales-biased content Buyer-reported trust confidence (survey) Perception metric to catch problems before they show up in churn 10. Product Philosophy Eco Swadesh treats trust as the product , not a feature of the product. Every other capability — commerce, community, AI advisory — exists to generate, display, or protect verifiable trust signals between people who have never met and may never meet in person, often across borders and certification regimes. This is closer in spirit to how a credit bureau or an escrow service thinks about its core asset than how a typical e-commerce app does. 11. Brand Positioning For ethical consumers, diaspora buyers, and sustainability-minded businesses who want to buy verified organic and sustainable goods, and for smallholder producers, home growers, and independent experts who want fair, global market access — Eco Swadesh is the trusted global ecosystem for sustainable agriculture that verifies claims other marketplaces take on faith. Unlike generic marketplaces (Amazon, Alibaba, Etsy) or single-purpose agri-apps (BigHaat, AgroStar, Plantix), Eco Swadesh unifies verified commerce, neutral expertise, and global community in one identity-centric platform. 12. Unique Selling Proposition “ See the proof, not just the label. ” Every organic or sustainability claim on Eco Swadesh is backed by a certification object with an issuer, a scope, and an expiry — visible to the buyer before purchase, not asserted after the fact. 13. The Act is phased: Phase I (Data Protection Board established) took effect November 13, 2025; Phase II (consent-manager framework) takes effect November 13, 2026; Phase III (full substantive compliance obligations) takes effect May 13, 2027 , giving the team a defined runway but not an excuse to defer privacy-by-design work. - Unlike GDPR, the DPDP Act relies primarily on consent and a narrower set of “ legitimate uses ” as lawful bases, rather than GDPR’s six lawful bases, and defines a child as anyone under 18 versus GDPR’s under-16 threshold — the product’s consent flows and any minor-related safeguards must be built to the stricter (DPDP) age threshold to remain compliant in both regimes simultaneously without region-specific logic branching. - Cross-border data transfer under DPDP is comparatively permissive today — personal data may generally be transferred outside India except to specific government-blacklisted countries or entities, unlike GDPR’s more restrictive Chapter V regime — but the architecture should still support region-pinned data residency as a configurable capability, anticipating EU expansion. - [Assumption] GDPR and CCPA-specific requirements (e.g., GDPR’s broader lawful-basis model, right to erasure nuances, CCPA’s “ sale of data ” opt-outs) will be fully specified in a dedicated compliance addendum once a EU/US launch is scheduled; this SRS only requires that the data model not preclude that future work. 3.2.7 Data Retention, Backup, Disaster Recovery, Monitoring Data retention policy must be explicit per data category (transaction records, certification records, chat/community content, AI interaction logs) and documented before launch, since DPDP’s breach-notification obligations require organizations to know what they hold. Automated daily backups of Firestore data; documented RPO/RTO targets to be finalized in Document 8 (System Architecture). UC-10 — Ask and Answer a Community Question Goal: A user gets neutral, non-sales-biased guidance from peers or verified experts. Primary Actor: Buyer, Seller (asking); Expert (answering) Secondary Actors: Moderator (content moderation) Preconditions: Authenticated account. Main Flow: User posts a question, optionally tagging category/crop type. Community members and/or verified Experts respond. System visually distinguishes Expert answers (credential badge) from general community answers. Asker (or community) can mark an answer as helpful/accepted. Alternative Flow: 2a. Expert offers a paid 1:1 consultation (Document 2, §17 monetization layer) — routes to a separate scheduling/payment flow, out of scope for this use case’s detail. Exception Flow: 2b. Content flagged as spam/inappropriate — routes to UC-12 (moderation). Business Rules: Expert status requires a credentialing step (not detailed here — see Document 5 for the expert-onboarding story) distinct from ordinary registration (UC-01). Postconditions: Question thread exists with responses, visibly attributed. UC-12 — Moderate a Flagged Listing or Post Goal: A Moderator or Administrator resolves a flagged item to maintain marketplace/community trust. Primary Actor: Moderator, Administrator Secondary Actors: System (flag queue), Support Executive (if buyer/seller communication needed) Preconditions: An item has been flagged by a user or an automated rule. Main Flow: Moderator opens moderation queue, selects a flagged item. Moderator reviews content/context and evidence. Moderator takes action: dismiss flag, remove content, suspend account, or escalate to Administrator for severe cases. System logs the decision and notifies affected user(s) with reasoning. Alternative Flow: 3a. Moderator requests more information from the flagged user before deciding. Exception Flow: 3b. Repeated violations by same user — System auto-flags account for Administrator review of suspension/ban. Business Rules: All moderation actions are audit-logged (consistent with SRS FR-11’s certification-audit principle, generalized to all trust-affecting admin actions). Postconditions: Flag resolved; content/account state updated; audit trail recorded. 5.
